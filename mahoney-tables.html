How to use this

The method in four steps. Each step feeds the next, and every step below shows the reference table it used, so you can check any result by hand.
  1. Table 1 — put in twelve months of temperature, humidity and rainfall. The app works out the monthly range, the annual figures, and each month's humidity group.
  2. Table 2 — comfort limits are looked up from the annual mean temperature and the humidity group, then compared with the monthly means to label each day and night Hot, cOmfortable or Cold.
  3. Indicators — six yes/no tests are applied to every month. The six totals (0–12) are the entire input to the recommendations.
  4. Tables 3 and 4 — the totals select sketch-design and detailed-design guidance.

Blue-outlined cells are editable. Paste a row of twelve values from Excel straight into the first cell of any row.

Reference — humidity groups

The average relative humidity row above decides each month's group. The group is what selects the comfort limits in Table 2, so it drives everything downstream.

Boundaries are read as: 30% falls in group 2, 50% in group 2, 70% in group 3. A month only leaves a group once it is strictly above the figure shown.

What the other rows are for
  • Monthly mean maximum / minimum — the average of the daily highs and the average of the daily lows for that month. Not the single hottest and coldest hour.
  • Monthly mean range — the difference between them: how far the temperature swings between day and night in a typical day of that month. Indicators H1 and A1 both test it against 10 K.
  • AMT, annual mean temperature — midway between the year's highest monthly mean maximum and its lowest monthly mean minimum. It picks which of the three comfort tables applies.
  • AMR, annual mean range — the difference between those same two figures. Reported for reference; the indicators do not use it.
  • Rainfall — monthly total in mm. Only indicator H3 uses it, at a threshold of 200 mm.
  • Wind — recorded for the designer's benefit. The Mahoney indicators make no use of it, but it tells you which way to face the openings that Table 4 asks for.

Reference  Comfort limits

This is the lookup table Table 2 reads. The highlighted block is the one your annual mean temperature selects; the highlighted rows are the humidity groups your months actually fall into.

How a month is classified

Two comparisons are made for every month, using the limits from that month's humidity group:

  • Day — the monthly mean maximum is compared with the day limits. Above the upper limit it is H; between the two it is O; below the lower limit it is C.
  • Night — the monthly mean minimum is compared the same way with the night limits.

A value sitting exactly on a limit counts as inside comfort.

Comfort profile

Monthly temperature against the comfort band for that month's humidity group.

What the three views show
  • Range — the two numbers the Mahoney method actually uses: the monthly mean maximum and the monthly mean minimum, joined by a line. Available from typed-in data alone.
  • Box — a candlestick of every hour in the month: the box spans the middle half of all hours (25th to 75th percentile), the whiskers reach the 5th and 95th, and the pale caps mark the absolute extremes. The bar across the box is the median.
  • Leaf — the same hours drawn as a distribution. The width at any height is how much of the month sits at that temperature, so a fat waist means a steady month and a long thin leaf a volatile one.

Box and Leaf need an EPW file, because they describe all 8,760 hours rather than twelve pairs of numbers. They also legitimately reach beyond the Range view: a mean maximum is an average of the daily highs, so individual hours run hotter and colder than it.

Psychrometric chart

Temperature and humidity together, month by month, against the Mahoney comfort zone.

How to read it, and how the comfort zone is built
  • The horizontal axis is dry-bulb temperature; the vertical axis is humidity ratio — the actual mass of water carried per kilogram of dry air. The curves sweeping up to the right are lines of constant relative humidity, and the topmost is saturation, 100%. Air cannot exist above it.
  • Daily loop draws each month's average day as a closed 24-hour path. It leans to the right through the afternoon as the air heats and its relative humidity falls, and returns to the left overnight. A tall loop means moisture is being added or removed across the day — usually rain or sea breeze; a flat loop means the air simply heats and cools the same parcel of moisture.
  • Range box replaces each loop with the 5th-to-95th percentile spread of all hours in the month.
  • The shaded zone is the Mahoney comfort zone. It is stepped, not a smooth blob, because the method itself is stepped: each humidity group has its own temperature limits, so the band narrows as you climb into more humid air. Its horizontal edges are the night lower limit and the day upper limit for that group, taken from the comfort reference table above.
  • Months whose loops sit to the right of the zone need cooling and air movement; below and to the left, warming. A loop sitting above the zone but at the right temperature is telling you the problem is moisture, not heat.

Humidity ratio is computed with the ASHRAE saturation-pressure formulation at a fixed 101.325 kPa, so charts from different altitudes stay comparable.

Method notes

How EPW data is reduced to the twelve monthly figures
  • Monthly mean maximum / minimum temperature — the daily maximum and minimum dry-bulb temperature are found for each day, then averaged over each month. This is the definition the Mahoney method assumes; it is not the same as the highest and lowest hour in the month.
  • Relative humidity — the Mahoney tables want a morning (high) and afternoon (low) reading. With Fixed hours the app averages the 07:00 and 15:00 values across each month; with Daily max/min it averages each day's highest and lowest RH. The "average" row is the mean of the two, exactly as the original method specifies, and it alone sets the humidity group.
  • Rainfall — EPW field 34, liquid precipitation depth, summed per month. Many EPW files leave this field missing (999) or all-zero; when that happens the app tells you and you must enter monthly rainfall by hand from another source. Rainfall drives indicator H3 and every rain-protection recommendation, so do not leave it wrong.
  • Wind — the most and second-most frequent 16-point compass sector, counting only hours above 0.5 m/s.
  • Hourly aggregates — for the Box, Leaf and psychrometric views the app keeps a compact summary of the hourly data: a 1 K temperature histogram, a set of percentiles, and the mean 24-hour temperature and humidity-ratio profile for each month. The hourly file itself is never stored or transmitted.
Differences from the supplied spreadsheet

This app follows the published Koenigsberger/Mahoney method. Four points where the spreadsheet departs from it:

  • Broken A3 reference. In both Table 3 and Table 4 the A3 total reads ='Table 2 Diagnosis'!TP27 — a typo for P27. A3 therefore always evaluates as zero in the spreadsheet, silently corrupting the layout, opening-size and sun-protection recommendations in any climate with cold months. Ahmedabad has A3 = 0, so the error is invisible there.
  • Comfort limit typo. Table 2 cell F4 (humidity group 1, annual mean over 20 °C, night lower limit) holds 14; the published value is 17. Rows 5–7 correctly hold 17.
  • Comfort band edges. The spreadsheet classifies a month as cold when the mean equals the lower comfort limit, because it tests >. This app treats a value equal to a limit as within comfort.
  • Annual mean temperature bands. The spreadsheet sends an annual mean of exactly 15 °C to the "below 15" band; the published table puts 15–20 inclusive in the middle band, which is what this app does.

Everything else — the comfort table, all six indicator rules, and all thirty-three recommendation rules — matches the spreadsheet.
